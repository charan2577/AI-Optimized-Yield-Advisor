1. Executive Summary
Semiconductor test generates terabytes of per-die parametric and functional data every week, yet
most of it is consumed only as pass/fail bins and weekly yield charts. Yield loss is found late, root
cause takes days of manual commonality analysis, test limits are static compromises between
overkill and escapes, and test programs carry redundant items that cost tester time on every unit.
The Yield Advisor turns that data into ranked, explainable, actionable recommendations. It
combines five AI engines over a shared die-level data foundation:
  Wafer Map Pattern AI classifies spatial failure signatures (edge ring, scratch, center, cluster
and others) on every wafer within seconds of sort completion and links each signature to its
likely process or probe cause.
 Excursion Detector flags yield and parametric shifts with EWMA/CUSUM and multivariate
anomaly models, catching small sustained shifts that Shewhart charts miss.
 Root Cause Engine ranks equipment, chamber, recipe, probe card, tester, and material factors
by their contribution to the loss using gradient-boosted models with SHAP attribution and
classical commonality statistics.
 Adaptive Limits and Outlier Screening applies Dynamic Part Average Testing (DPAT),
Good-Die-in-Bad-Neighborhood (GDBN/NNR), and multivariate Mahalanobis screens to remove
latent-defect die that pass specification limits.
 Test Time Optimizer identifies redundant or low-coverage test items and proposes sampling or
removal under an explicit, statistically bounded escape risk.
An Advisor layer scores every candidate action by expected yield or cost benefit, confidence, and
quality risk, then presents it with evidence. An LLM copilot grounded with retrieval over historical
8D reports, failure analysis, and test program documentation explains findings in plain language
and drafts change requests. No test program, limit, or disposition changes without human approval
through the existing change control process.
 Bottom line: for a product line running 10,000 wafers a month, recovering 1.5 yield points and
cutting 10% of test time is worth on the order of USD 9 to 10 million per year (worked example
in Section 12), with a 12-month phased rollout starting from a read-only pilot.

3. Yield Fundamentals Used by the System
The Advisor's quantitative reasoning rests on standard yield definitions so that its outputs reconcile
with existing yield reports.
3.1 Definitions
Metric
Definition
Wafer sort yield
Ysort = good die / gross die per wafer (GDPW), per wafer, lot, and product
Line-to-ship yield
Ytotal = Yline x Ysort x Yassy x YFT x YSLT
Systematic vs. random
Ysort = Ysys x Yrand. Spatial patterns and parametric shifts drive Ysys; distributed defects
drive Yrand
Overkill
Good die rejected by test (false fail). Estimated from retest recovery, guard-band analysis,
and downstream correlation
Underkill (escape)
Defective die passed by test. Measured as DPPM from downstream fails and field returns
Test escape DPPM
DPPM = escapes / units shipped x 106
Retest recovery rate
Share of first-pass fails that pass on retest, by bin. High rates indicate contact or limit
issues
3.2 Random-defect yield models
For a die of critical area A and defect density D0, the Advisor fits the negative binomial model,
which reduces to Poisson as the clustering parameter grows:
 Yrand = (1 + A D0 / α)-α
Fitting D0 and α per wafer from the non-patterned fail density lets the system separate the random
component from systematic loss. That split decides who owns the recovery: the fab (defect
density) or test and product engineering (systematic and test-induced loss).

5.3 Die identity and genealogy
Each die carries a canonical key (fab, lot, wafer, x, y). Where the design includes an
electronic chip ID (ECID) fuse, it is read at sort and at final test so that FT, SLT, and field results join
back to the exact wafer location. Without ECID, the system uses assembly lot traceability and
reports joins as probabilistic, with that uncertainty carried into any downstream model.

Volume planning: a 10,000 wafers per month line at 600 GDPW and 2,000 parametric tests per die
produces about 12 billion test values a month. Columnar storage with dictionary encoding brings
this to roughly 1.5 to 3 TB per month for the test_value table.
5.5 Data quality gates
  Schema and record-order validation for every STDF file; files with missing PRR or truncated
records are quarantined.
 Limit consistency: program revision and limits are versioned; a value is never compared against
limits from a different revision.
 Coordinate normalization to a common notch-down orientation before any spatial model.
 Duplicate and retest handling: every insertion is kept, flagged by insertion number, and a
final-disposition view is derived.
 Completeness monitors per tester and site so that missing data is never mistaken for a yield
change.

6.1 Wafer Map Pattern AI
Task. Classify each wafer's pass/fail map (and optionally per-bin maps) into failure signature
classes, localize the pattern, and estimate the die lost to it.

Model. A convolutional network (ResNet-18 scale) on maps resampled to 64 x 64, with a
three-channel input (fail, bin class group, die-valid mask). A vision transformer variant is evaluated
in the pilot. Mixed-type wafers (two patterns at once) are handled as multi-label with sigmoid
outputs; a segmentation head (U-Net) outlines the pattern region so lost die can be counted.
Training data. Public WM-811K (811,457 maps, of which about 172,950 are labeled into 9 classes)
and MixedWM38 for pre-training, then fine-tuned on in-house labeled wafers. Class imbalance (the
majority are 'none') is handled with focal loss, rotation and flip augmentation (patterns are
rotation-equivariant), and synthetic pattern injection.

Open-set handling. New signatures must not be forced into known classes. An energy-score

Targets. Macro F1 at or above 0.93 on in-house hold-out, at least 0.85 recall on each rare class,
classification under 200 ms per wafer on CPU.
6.2 Excursion Detector
Task. Detect statistically meaningful shifts in yield, bin rates, and key parametric distributions at
the earliest possible lot.
Figure 4. A sustained shift of about 1.7σ begins at lot 78. EWMA accumulates evidence across lots; a Shewhart chart
reacts only to single extreme points. On real data EWMA typically signals small shifts several lots earlier.
Methods. The detector layers several complementary checks:
  Univariate EWMA and CUSUM charts on yield, each top soft bin, and the median and spread of
critical parametrics, by product, tester, and probe card.
 Multivariate monitoring with Hotelling T2 on PCA-reduced parametric signatures per wafer,
catching shifts spread across many tests that are individually in control.
 Isolation Forest on wafer-level feature vectors (pattern probabilities, bin mix, parametric
moments) for novel anomalies.
 Change-point detection (PELT) for retrospective dating of when a shift began, which narrows the
root cause search window.
False alarm control uses Benjamini-Hochberg across the many simultaneous charts, so engineers
are not flooded. Alarm thresholds target an in-control average run length of about 500 lots per
chart family.
6.3 Root Cause Engine
Task. Given an excursion or a persistent loss signature, rank the factors most likely responsible.
Commonality analysis. For every process step, compare the distribution of affected vs.
unaffected wafers across equipment and chambers using chi-square or Fisher exact tests, and
Kruskal-Wallis for continuous responses. This is the classical method engineers already trust and it
anchors the ranking.

Model-based attribution. A gradient-boosted tree model (LightGBM) predicts wafer yield loss or
signature probability from routing, equipment, timing (queue times), WAT, and probe context.
SHAP values give each factor's contribution per wafer and in aggregate. Interaction values expose
combinations such as 'chamber B only after a PM'.
Causal guardrails. Equipment assignment is confounded with time and product mix. The engine
adjusts for these with stratification and, for important decisions, runs a double machine learning
estimate of the effect of a suspected tool with time and product as controls. Each ranked factor is
reported with effect size, p-value, wafers affected, and the time window, and is labeled
'association' unless a split-lot or tool-down experiment confirms it.

6.4 Adaptive Test Limits and Outlier Screening
Task. Remove die that pass specification limits but are statistically abnormal (latent reliability
risk), and right-size guard bands to cut overkill.
Figure 5. DPAT on one wafer. Limits computed from the wafer's own robust distribution flag outliers that sit well
inside the specification limits.
Part Average Testing. Following the AEC-Q001 guideline, static PAT limits are set at a robust
mean plus or minus 6 robust sigma from qualification data; dynamic PAT recomputes them per
wafer or lot:
 robust_mean  = median(x)
robust_sigma = (Q3 - Q1) / 1.35
DPAT limits  = robust_mean +/- k * robust_sigma      (k = 6 default, tuned per test)
apply only if within spec:  max(LSL, low)  ...  min(USL, high)
 Good Die in Bad Neighborhood (GDBN). A passing die surrounded by failing die carries
elevated latent defect risk. The engine computes a weighted neighbor fail score and also applies

 Nearest Neighbor Residual (NNR). For spatially trending parametrics such as IDDQ, a die's
value is compared with the median of its neighbors rather than with the whole wafer, which
catches outliers that wafer-level PAT hides.
 Multivariate PAT. Robust Mahalanobis distance (Minimum Covariance Determinant) across

 Guard-band optimization. Using retest data and downstream correlation, the engine
estimates measurement repeatability per test and proposes guard bands sized to the measured
gauge uncertainty instead of legacy fixed margins. This is the main lever for recovering overkill.
Calibration to quality. Every screen is evaluated on yield cost and DPPM benefit, using burn-in
and field return labels where available. A screen is recommended only if its expected DPPM
reduction per yield point lost exceeds the product's quality-cost threshold.
6.5 Test Time Optimizer
Task. Reduce tester seconds per unit without raising escapes.
Figure 6. Candidate test items for sampling or removal in an example flow. Removal is proposed only when
unique-fail coverage is below the risk bound.
 Redundancy analysis. For each test, count 'unique fails': die that fail only this test. Tests with
near-zero unique fails over millions of units are candidates. Correlated parametrics (r > 0.98)
suggest one test can predict another.
 Risk bound. With zero unique fails in n units, the 95% upper bound on the escape rate from
removing a test is about 3/n (rule of three). Removal is proposed only when this bound is below
the DPPM budget allocated to test optimization.
 Adaptive test. A per-die model predicts pass probability from early tests and sort history;
low-risk die skip expensive items while a random audit sample (for example 2%) always runs the
full flow to keep the model honest.
 Test reordering. Tests with high fail probability per second run earlier so that failing die stop
sooner under stop-on-fail.

7. Advisor Layer: Recommendation Logic
Each engine emits candidate actions. The Advisor normalizes them into one inbox ranked by
risk-adjusted value, so a yield engineer sees the most valuable decision first, whatever engine
produced it.
7.1 Scoring
 score = P(correct) x expected_benefit_usd  -  expected_quality_cost_usd  -  effort_cost_usd
expected_benefit_usd   = recovered_die/month x die_value  +  tester_sec_saved x cost_per_sec
expected_quality_cost  = delta_DPPM x units x cost_per_escape   (escape cost set by customer tier)
P(correct)             = calibrated model confidence (isotonic regression on past outcomes)
Confidence is calibrated against the outcome of previous recommendations, so a stated 80%
means roughly four in five similar recommendations were confirmed. Actions that touch shipped
quality (limit widening, test removal) also need the DPPM bound check from Section 6.5 to pass
before they can appear.

7.3 LLM copilot
A language model makes the evidence accessible: engineers ask questions such as 'why did lot
2214 drop on SB07?' and receive an answer assembled from engine outputs, charts, and similar
past cases.
  Grounding. Retrieval over 8D reports, FA results, test program release notes, and specification
documents, indexed by product and signature. Every claim in an answer links to its source
record.
 Tool use. The copilot calls the same query APIs as the UI (yield by factor, wafer maps,
commonality runs). It never calculates statistics itself; numbers come from the engines.
 Drafting. It drafts 8D D2 to D4 sections, change request justifications, and shift handover
summaries for human editing.
 Guardrails. Read-only data access, no direct write path to test programs or MES, refusal when
evidence is insufficient, and logging of every prompt and response for audit.
8. Human-in-the-Loop and Governance
Test programs and limits are controlled quality documents, and in automotive and medical markets
they are tied to customer-approved control plans. The Advisor fits that process rather than
bypassing it.
  Autonomy levels. Level 0 (inform), Level 1 (recommend, human executes), Level 2
(auto-execute within a pre-approved policy, such as retest of contact bins up to a cap).
Production starts at Level 1; only explicitly approved policies run at Level 2.
 Change control. Limit and content changes generate a change request with evidence pack,
risk bound, and rollback plan, routed to the existing change control board. Automotive products
follow PPAP and customer notification rules.
 Traceability. Every recommendation stores model version, data snapshot, and decision, so any
shipped unit's test conditions can be reconstructed.
 Feedback. Accept, reject, and outcome labels feed retraining and confidence calibration.
Rejection reasons are mandatory picklists so the system learns why.

10. Deployment and Integration
10.1 Topology
  Edge. One agent per tester cell or per group of testers, on a small server on the test floor

dedicated cloud VPC. Multi-site deployments keep raw data in region and replicate only
aggregates when data residency requires it.
 OSAT and foundry data. Partners deliver STDF through secured transfer; a partner-facing
ingest validates and maps it to the canonical schema.

  Test data reveals design and process IP. All data is encrypted in transit (TLS 1.3) and at rest

respects the same access controls as the UI.
